Machine Copy

Load an ink painting. The program separates it into washes (broad areas of ink) and strokes (lines whose width it measures along their length), then paints both with the same simulated ink as Splashed Ink: wet ink first, then dry-brush strokes, then lines.

Load a painting to begin. Public-domain works from museum open-access collections are a good source.

How it works

Washes are the ink that is wider than a brush line. A broad stroke is dragged along the centre of each wash, as wide as the wash is at every point, and it is painted wet or dry depending on the image: wide, dark, smooth areas are laid as wet ink, while narrower or streaky ones are dragged with little water so the bristle marks and white gaps stay. Wet areas go down first and dry strokes are laid over them once they have settled. Round pours fill in whatever the strokes leave uncovered. The sizes come from a short calibration run on blank paper. Strokes are the thin dark structures left over. Each is thinned to a centre line, and the distance to the edge gives its width at every point. The brush then travels along that line, wide where the original is wide, thinning to the tip. Lighter strokes are drawn before darker ones, and a stroke starts at its thicker end. How dry a line runs (the broken white streaks) is measured where the original shows gaps along it, at the image's own resolution, and each stroke is also assumed to run drier toward its tip. Wide strokes are painted wet or dry from how wide, dark and streaky the original is; in a painting whose big areas are smooth wet ink, most of them will be wet and the dry-brush contrast comes from the lines.
